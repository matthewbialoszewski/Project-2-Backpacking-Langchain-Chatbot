# Layering for Backpacking

The layering system lets you regulate temperature across changing conditions without carrying redundant clothing. Three layers cover most three-season trips.

## Base Layer
Sits against your skin and manages moisture. Choose merino wool for warmth and odor resistance, or synthetic (polyester) for faster drying and lower cost. Avoid cotton — it holds moisture, loses insulation when wet, and dries slowly. "Cotton kills" is a real saying for a reason.

Typical weight: 150 g/m^2 for warmer trips, 200 g/m^2 for colder.

## Mid Layer (Insulation)
Traps body heat. Two main options:
- Down: Best warmth-to-weight ratio. Useless when wet. Look for 800+ fill power.
- Synthetic: Heavier and bulkier than down, but insulates when damp. Better for wet climates like the Pacific Northwest or Appalachians.

A fleece can substitute for synthetic insulation on warmer trips and breathes better while moving.

## Shell Layer
Blocks wind and rain. Hardshells (Gore-Tex, eVent) are durable and fully waterproof but expensive and less breathable. A lightweight wind shell plus a separate rain jacket is a common ultralight setup.

## What to Wear vs. Pack
A general rule: dress for the second mile, not the first. You'll warm up quickly while hiking. If you start comfortable, you'll be sweating in 20 minutes.

## Sleep Clothes
Always carry one dry set of base layers reserved only for sleeping. If your hiking clothes are soaked, sleep clothes can save your night and possibly your life.

## Common Mistakes
- Wearing cotton t-shirts or jeans
- Bringing one heavy jacket instead of layers
- Forgetting a wind shell (lightweight, cheap, hugely effective)
- Skipping a beanie and gloves — small, light, and crucial for cold mornings
